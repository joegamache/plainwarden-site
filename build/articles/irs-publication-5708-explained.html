IRS Publication 5708, "Creating a Written Information Security Plan for your Tax & Accounting Practice,", is a free guide with a sample plan. The Security Summit, a partnership of the IRS, state tax agencies and private-sector tax groups, wrote it for tax professionals, especially smaller practices. As of November 2026, the IRS links to the August 2024 revision, which runs 28 pages.

It is a good starting point. It is not a finished plan, and it says so: the publication is "not intended to replace your own research" or to substitute for a plan built on your firm's own needs.

What's in the publication

  • Requirements: a summary of what the FTC Safeguards Rule asks of tax and accounting professionals, who are treated as financial institutions regardless of size.
  • Getting started: scale the plan to your firm, and cover physical, technical and administrative safeguards. The IRS gives the example of a sole practitioner using a simpler plan than a 10-partner firm.
  • An outline of the bare essentials of a plan.
  • A sample template you can adapt.
  • Added detail on how to write each part.
  • Six sample attachments, a glossary and resource links.

The seven sections of the sample plan

  1. Objective.
  2. Purpose.
  3. Scope.
  4. Identified responsible officials: a Data Security Coordinator, who runs the plan, and a Public Information Officer, who speaks for the firm. The publication says that in most firms of two or more practitioners, these should be different people.
  5. Inside the firm risk mitigation: collecting and keeping personal information, staff accountability, disclosure and reportable events.
  6. Outside the firm risk mitigation: network protection, user access, electronic exchange, Wi-Fi, remote access, connected devices and training.
  7. Implementation: the effective date and signatures of the owner or principal officer and the Coordinator.

The attachments

  • A: Record retention. Destruction methods, plus a blank for the longest you will keep records.
  • B: Rules of behavior. Staff rules on email, links, devices, passwords and browsing.
  • C: Security breach procedures. Who to notify, including your IRS Stakeholder Liaison, state agencies, the FTC, law enforcement, your software vendor, your insurer and affected clients.
  • D: Acknowledgment. A form each employee or contractor signs after training.
  • E: Hardware inventory. A table of devices that hold personal information.
  • F: Authorized access. A table of who can reach personal information, and at what level.

What it leaves to you

  • Your facts. The sample is written as statements about what the firm does. For example, it says the firm will use multi-factor authentication for remote login. If you keep a sentence you don't follow, your plan says something untrue. Change the practice, or write the gap down with a date to fix it.
  • Your risk assessment. The publication tells you to list the information you handle, how it could be lost and how you will monitor for new risks, but it has no risk assessment form. Every firm has to assess its risks under 16 CFR 314.4(b). Firms with information on 5,000 or more consumers must also put the assessment in writing, with the criteria the rule lists. Section 314.6 excepts smaller firms from that written requirement.
  • Your retention periods. Attachment A leaves the number blank. Under 16 CFR 314.4(c)(6), customer information must be securely disposed of no later than two years after it was last used to serve the customer, unless you need it for business or legal reasons or targeted disposal isn't reasonably feasible. Set periods that fit your records, and confirm them with your adviser. Our retention and disposal guide covers the mechanics.
  • Your breach contacts. Attachment C says who to notify, but finding contacts during an incident is the wrong time to start. Write down your Stakeholder Liaison, state agencies, insurer and software vendor now, then walk through the checklist once a year.
  • The rest of the rule. The publication says it isn't an exhaustive discussion, so check your finished plan against the Safeguards Rule elements, including two-step login for anyone accessing an information system, and service provider oversight.
  • Upkeep. The sample calls for review at least once a year. The IRS calls a plan an evergreen document, to be reviewed, tested and updated as your firm changes.

How to work through it

  1. Read the Requirements and Getting Started pages first.
  2. Fill in Attachments E and F. Knowing your devices and who has access makes the rest easier.
  3. Go through the template sentence by sentence. Keep what's true, rewrite what isn't, and list what you need to fix.
  4. Name your Coordinator and Public Information Officer.
  5. Write your risk assessment, and fill in your retention periods and breach contacts.
  6. Sign Section VII, and have each person sign the acknowledgment after training.
  7. Keep a readable copy where you can reach it. The publication recommends storing one offsite or in the cloud, since your computers may be what's affected. Put the yearly review on your calendar.

Does using the sample make you compliant?

Not by itself. The sample helps you document your program, but what counts is whether the safeguards exist and whether your plan describes them accurately. Questions about how the rules apply to your firm are ones to take to your adviser.

Publication 5708 or PlainWarden?

If you have the time to adapt the sample carefully, Publication 5708 is free and written for practices like yours. Start there. PlainWarden is for firms that would rather answer questions than edit a template: about 20 minutes of questions produce a plan organized around the same sections, with attachments that also include a draft risk assessment. Each safeguard is marked as reported in place, a gap or something to confirm. Like the sample, it doesn't certify compliance. See a sample plan, or use the WISP checklist either way.
