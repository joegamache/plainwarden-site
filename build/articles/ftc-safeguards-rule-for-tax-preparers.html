The FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314) tells financial institutions, a category that includes paid tax preparers, how to protect customer information. Here is what each part asks of a practice, followed by the limited exceptions for firms maintaining information on fewer than 5,000 consumers.

The nine elements (16 CFR 314.4)

  1. A Qualified Individual oversees the program. It can be an owner, an employee or an outside provider, but the firm stays responsible.
  2. A written risk assessment identifies foreseeable risks to customer information and how well current safeguards address them.
  3. Safeguards that control the risks, including:
    • access limited to people who need it, with periodic reviews;
    • an inventory of data, devices and systems;
    • encryption of customer information at rest and in transit, or, if encryption is infeasible, effective alternative compensating controls reviewed and approved by the Qualified Individual;
    • secure development practices for in-house applications and security evaluation of external applications that transmit, access or store customer information;
    • multi-factor authentication for anyone accessing an information system, unless the Qualified Individual approves reasonably equivalent or more secure controls in writing;
    • secure disposal no later than two years after the information was last used, with some exceptions;
    • change management;
    • monitoring and logging of user activity.
  4. Testing and monitoring: regular testing or monitoring of safeguards. For firms subject to §314.4(d)(2), absent effective continuous monitoring or other ongoing vulnerability-detection systems, this includes annual penetration testing and vulnerability assessments at least every six months, plus assessments after material changes or circumstances.
  5. Staff training and qualified security personnel, whether in-house or through a provider.
  6. Service provider oversight: choose capable providers, require safeguards by contract and check on them periodically.
  7. Keeping the program current as your business, technology and threats change.
  8. A written incident response plan.
  9. A yearly written report from the Qualified Individual to the owner or board.

If you maintain information on fewer than 5,000 consumers

The threshold is customer information you maintain concerning fewer than 5,000 consumers, not just current-year clients or returns. Section 314.6 excepts only §314.4(b)(1), (d)(2), (h) and (i): specified written risk-assessment provisions, the prescribed information-system testing regime, a written incident response plan and the annual written report. You still need a written security program, a risk assessment and periodic reassessment under §314.4(b), and regular testing or monitoring under §314.4(d)(1). Other requirements remain, including multi-factor authentication, encryption, training, vendor oversight and applicable breach notification. Confirm your firm's obligations with your adviser.

Breach notice to the FTC

Since May 2024, a notification event involving unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted customer information of at least 500 consumers must be reported to the FTC as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery (16 CFR 314.4(j)). State breach laws may separately require notice to clients.

Where the IRS fits

The IRS points preparers to the same rule and publishes practical help: Publication 4557 on safeguarding taxpayer data, and Publication 5708, a sample written plan for small practices. Internal Revenue Code Section 7216 rules on using and disclosing tax return information apply alongside it.
