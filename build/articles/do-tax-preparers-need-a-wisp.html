Yes. If you prepare tax returns for pay, you need a written information security plan, often called a WISP. It applies to solo preparers working from home as much as to larger firms.

Why it applies to you

Under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, professional tax preparers are treated as financial institutions. That puts them under the FTC Standards for Safeguarding Customer Information, known as the Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314), which requires a written information security program. The IRS reinforces this: preparers agree to protect taxpayer data when they get a PTIN, and IRS Publication 4557 and the PTIN renewal both remind preparers that a written data security plan is required. The IRS also publishes Publication 5708, a sample plan, to help small practices create one.

What the plan has to cover

  • A Qualified Individual who runs the program. In a small firm this is usually the owner, called the Data Security Coordinator.
  • A written risk assessment of what could go wrong with client data.
  • Safeguards such as access controls, encryption, two-step login, secure disposal, change management and activity monitoring.
  • Staff training and oversight of service providers such as your tax software, email and IT provider.
  • An incident response plan, and notice to the FTC within 30 days of discovering a security event involving unencrypted information of 500 or more consumers.

Firms that keep information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from a few requirements, including the written risk assessment, the written incident response plan and the yearly report to the owner. They still need the rest of the program. Many small firms keep a short risk assessment and incident checklist anyway, because they make the rest of the plan easier to follow.

What a plan is, and what it isn't

A plan describes what your firm actually does to protect client data and what it will fix. A template that checks every box without saying which safeguards are really in place can leave you worse off: it documents promises you aren't keeping. The useful version is honest about gaps and gives each one an owner and a date.

How to get it done

  1. List where client data lives: tax software, email, cloud storage, computers, phones and paper.
  2. Write down what protections are in place for each, and what isn't.
  3. Name your Coordinator and set target dates for the gaps.
  4. Train staff, sign the plan and review it at least once a year.
