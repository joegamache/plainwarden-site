Turn on two-step login

Require a second step, such as an authenticator app code, whenever anyone signs in to a system that holds client information.

Why it matters

A stolen password is the most common way into a small firm. Two-step login (multi-factor authentication) blocks most of those attempts, because the attacker also needs your phone or security key. The FTC Safeguards Rule requires it for anyone accessing any information system that holds customer information, unless your Qualified Individual approves an equivalent control in writing.

Steps

  1. List every system that holds client information: tax software, email, cloud storage, client portal, bookkeeping software, password manager, remote access and IRS e-Services.
  2. For each one, open the security or sign-in settings and turn on two-step login for every user, not just yourself. In business email (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace), the administrator can require it for all accounts.
  3. Use an authenticator app or a hardware security key where offered. Text-message codes are better than nothing, but easier to intercept.
  4. Save each account's backup codes somewhere safe, such as your password manager or a locked drawer, so a lost phone doesn't lock you out.
  5. If a system can't support two-step login, the FTC Safeguards Rule allows a reasonably equivalent or more secure control only if your Qualified Individual approves it in writing. A strong password alone is not equivalent. Record the control and the written approval, and plan to move off that system.
  6. Check again whenever you add a system or a person.

You're done when

  • Every system on your list shows two-step login on for every user.
  • Any exception is written down and approved by the Coordinator.

References: 16 CFR 314.4(c)(5); IRS Publication 4557
