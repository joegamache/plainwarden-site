Do I need a WISP if I work from home?

Working from home doesn't change whether you need a written information security plan. If you prepare income tax returns for pay, the FTC Safeguards Rule applies to you whether you work from a downtown office, a spare bedroom or the kitchen table.

What changes is what the plan has to describe. A home office has its own risks: shared computers, a home router, paper in a spare room, family and visitors nearby. Your plan should say how you handle each one.

Why size and location don't take you out of the rule

  • Tax preparers are covered. The Safeguards Rule lists "an accountant or other tax preparation service that is in the business of completing income tax returns" as a financial institution (16 CFR 314.2).
  • There is no size cutoff for the plan itself. The rule applies to all financial institutions under the FTC's jurisdiction (16 CFR 314.1) and requires a written program "appropriate to your size and complexity" (16 CFR 314.3). A one-person practice needs a plan; it can be a shorter one.
  • Small firms get a few exceptions, not an exemption. If you keep information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, 16 CFR 314.6 excepts you from the written risk assessment, the continuous monitoring or testing requirement, the written incident response plan and the yearly written report to your board or a senior officer. You still need the rest of the program, and you still need to assess your risks. The Safeguards Rule article goes through each element.

The IRS Office of Professional Responsibility makes the same point for remote work. Its July 2026 article on security plans says a plan should address the "safekeeping of physical files and other records kept at home."

If you work alone, you are the Qualified Individual

The rule requires one person to oversee the program, called the Qualified Individual (16 CFR 314.4(a)). Publication 5708's sample calls the role the Data Security Coordinator. In a solo practice, that's usually you. Write your name in the plan. The rule also lets an outside provider fill the role, but you stay responsible for the program, must oversee that person and must require the provider to keep its own security program (16 CFR 314.4(a)).

What your plan should say about a home office

The Safeguards Rule asks you to identify and manage the data, devices, systems and facilities you use (16 CFR 314.4(c)(2)). For a home-based practice, your home is one of those facilities. Cover these points.

  1. Which computer you use for client work. Only authorized users should be able to reach client information (16 CFR 314.4(c)(1)). A computer the family also uses makes that hard to show. Use a computer only you use for the practice, or at least a separate account that only you can sign in to. Keep it updated and protected; Publication 4557 asks firms to require virus protection on personal computers used for customer data. See keep computers updated and protected.
  2. Encryption. Client information must be encrypted at rest and in transit over external networks, or protected by alternative controls your Qualified Individual approves (16 CFR 314.4(c)(3)). Turn on full-disk encryption on the computer and any phone or drive that holds client files. See encrypt computers, phones and drives.
  3. Two-step login. Multi-factor authentication is required for anyone accessing any information system, unless your Qualified Individual approves an equivalent control in writing (16 CFR 314.4(c)(5)). That includes your tax software, email and cloud storage. See turn on two-step login.
  4. Your home network. Change the router's default password and keep its software current. Put family devices, smart TVs and guests on a separate network from your work computer. Publication 4557 says not to use public Wi-Fi, such as at a coffee shop or airport, to reach business email or sensitive documents. See secure your router and guest Wi-Fi.
  5. Remote access. If you connect to an office network or another firm's systems from home, Publication 4557 describes a secure virtual private network (VPN) as a minimum standard for remote access to the firm's office network.
  6. Paper. Keep client documents in a room or cabinet that is locked when you aren't there, and keep your desk clear when others are around. Publication 4557 recommends both. Shred paper with taxpayer information before throwing it away.
  7. Disposal and retention. The rule requires procedures to securely dispose of customer information in any format, generally no later than two years after you last used it to serve the client, unless you need to keep it longer for business or legal reasons (16 CFR 314.4(c)(6)). See set retention periods and dispose of records.
  8. Backups. Say where your backups are kept and whether they're encrypted. A backup drive on the same desk as the computer can be lost in the same fire or burglary. See back up client data and test a restore.
  9. Your vendors. A home practice often depends entirely on outside services: tax software, email, cloud storage and a client portal. The rule requires you to pick providers that can protect client data, require it in your contracts and check on them periodically (16 CFR 314.4(f)). See the vendor security guide.

If you have helpers working from their homes

Publication 4557 asks firms to set policies for employees who telecommute, including whether they may keep or access client data at home. Write down which devices they may use, whether client files may be stored on them, and how they connect. Give each person their own login and security awareness training (16 CFR 314.4(e)). See individual logins, access reviews and offboarding.

If something goes wrong

A stolen laptop or a break-in at home can be a security event. If unencrypted information about 500 or more consumers is acquired without authorization, you must notify the FTC as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery (16 CFR 314.4(j)). State breach notice laws may also apply; confirm with your adviser what your state requires. A short walk-through of your breach checklist helps you know what to do first.

Getting it written

Start with the IRS sample in Publication 5708 or the WISP checklist, and describe what you actually do at home. Where you don't yet meet a point above, list it as a gap with a target date. If you're renewing your PTIN, line 11 of Form W-12 asks you to confirm you know a plan is required. Whether your setup meets the rule depends on your facts; if you're unsure, check with your adviser.
