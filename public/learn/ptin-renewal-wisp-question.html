PTIN renewal and the WISP question: what line 11 asks

Form W-12 line 11 asks preparers to confirm they know a written security plan is required. What it says, what it doesn't, and what to check first.

PTIN renewal for 2027 is open. Current PTINs expire December 31, 2026, and the fee is $18.75. The IRS says renewing online takes about 15 minutes; a paper Form W-12 takes about six weeks to process.

Form W-12, the PTIN application and renewal form, includes a data security question on line 11. Here is what it asks, what it doesn't, and what to check before you answer it.

What line 11 says

Line 11 of Form W-12 (Rev. October 2026) is titled "Data Security Responsibilities." It reads: "I am aware that paid tax return preparers are required by law to create and maintain a written information security plan that provides data and system security protections for all taxpayer information." It then points you to IRS Publications 5708 and 4557. You must check Yes or No.

What you are confirming, and what you aren't

  • You are acknowledging awareness. The Form W-12 instructions describe line 11 as a box to "acknowledge awareness" that paid preparers must create and maintain a written plan.
  • You aren't sending the plan. The form doesn't ask you to attach a plan or describe it.
  • The requirement doesn't come from the form. It comes from the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314), which lists "an accountant or other tax preparation service that is in the business of completing income tax returns" as a financial institution (16 CFR 314.2). Covered firms must develop, implement and maintain a written information security program (16 CFR 314.3 and 314.4). For the rule element by element, see the Safeguards Rule in plain English.

So line 11 is a reminder, not the requirement itself. Checking Yes says you know the rule. Whether your firm actually has a plan, and follows it, is a separate question, and it's the one that matters after a breach.

Before you check Yes: a five-minute test

If you can answer yes to each of these, your plan is likely in good shape. Each item maps to a part of the Safeguards Rule.

  1. It's written down, and you can find it today (16 CFR 314.3).
  2. Someone is named to run it. The rule calls this person the Qualified Individual (16 CFR 314.4(a)). Publication 5708's sample calls the role the Data Security Coordinator.
  3. You have assessed your risks: where client data lives and what could go wrong (16 CFR 314.4(b)). Firms with information on fewer than 5,000 consumers don't need this in writing under 16 CFR 314.6, but they still need to do it.
  4. Multi-factor authentication is on for anyone who signs in to systems with client information (16 CFR 314.4(c)(5)). See the two-step login guide.
  5. Staff have had security training, updated as needed (16 CFR 314.4(e)).
  6. You oversee your service providers, such as your tax software, email and IT provider (16 CFR 314.4(f)). See the vendor security guide.
  7. You know what you'd do after a breach, including notifying the FTC within 30 days of discovering an event involving unencrypted information of 500 or more consumers (16 CFR 314.4(j)). A written incident response plan (16 CFR 314.4(h)) is one of the items 314.6 excepts for firms under 5,000 consumers. A short tabletop exercise helps either way.
  8. You've looked at it this year, and updated it for changes in your practice (16 CFR 314.4(g)).

The WISP checklist covers these in more detail.

If you don't have a plan yet

Start now. Renewal season is a good time to get it done before filing season. The IRS publishes Publication 5708, a sample plan for small practices, and Publication 4557, a guide to safeguarding taxpayer data. In its August 2026 reminder (IR-2026-92), the IRS says a good plan focuses on three areas: employee management and training, information systems, and detecting and managing system failures.

Write down what you actually do. If the sample says something your firm doesn't do yet, change the practice or list it as a gap with an owner and a date. A plan that claims safeguards you don't have is worse than an honest one with gaps.

Enrolled agents

PTIN renewal is separate from enrolled agent renewal. Enrolled agents need a 2027 PTIN too. The IRS says enrolled agents whose Social Security numbers end in 7, 8 or 9, and those without an SSN, are in the current enrollment renewal cycle, which runs through January 31, 2027.

Questions for your adviser

What happens to a preparer whose firm has no plan depends on the facts. An IRS Office of Professional Responsibility article from July 2026 notes that failing to keep a plan can expose a practitioner to liability under the Safeguards Rule and, in cases of willfulness, discipline under Circular 230. If you're unsure where your firm stands, confirm with your adviser.
