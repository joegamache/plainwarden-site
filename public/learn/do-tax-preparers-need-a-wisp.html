Do tax preparers need a written security plan?

Yes. Paid preparers are covered by the FTC Safeguards Rule and IRS rules. Here is who is covered, what the plan must contain and how to get one done.

Yes. If you prepare tax returns for pay, you need a written information security plan, often called a WISP. It applies to solo preparers working from home as much as to larger firms.

Why it applies to you

Under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, professional tax preparers are treated as financial institutions. That puts them under the FTC Standards for Safeguarding Customer Information, known as the Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314), which requires a written information security program. The IRS says the same thing plainly. In its August 2026 reminder (IR-2026-92), it states that federal law requires tax and accounting professionals to create and maintain a written plan. Line 11 of Form W-12, the PTIN application and renewal form, asks preparers to confirm they are aware of that requirement, and points them to Publications 5708 and 4557. The IRS also publishes Publication 5708, a sample plan, to help small practices create one. For the rule element by element, see the Safeguards Rule in plain English.

What the plan has to cover

  • A Qualified Individual who runs the program. It can be an owner, an employee or an outside provider. Publication 5708's sample calls this role the Data Security Coordinator.
  • A risk assessment of what could go wrong with client data, in writing if you keep information on 5,000 or more consumers.
  • Safeguards such as access controls, encryption, two-step login, secure disposal, change management and activity monitoring.
  • Staff training and oversight of service providers such as your tax software, email and IT provider.
  • An incident response plan, and notice to the FTC as soon as possible, and no later than 30 days after discovering, a security event involving unencrypted information of 500 or more consumers.

Firms that keep information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are excepted from a few requirements under 16 CFR 314.6: the written risk assessment, the continuous monitoring or testing requirement, the written incident response plan and the yearly report to the owner. They still need the rest of the program, including assessing their risks and reporting qualifying events to the FTC. Many small firms keep a short risk assessment and incident checklist anyway, because they make the rest of the plan easier to follow.

What a plan is, and what it isn't

The IRS says there is no one-size-fits-all plan. A plan describes what your firm actually does to protect client data and what it will fix. A template that checks every box without saying which safeguards are really in place can leave you worse off: it documents promises you aren't keeping. The useful version is honest about gaps and gives each one an owner and a date.

How to get it done

  1. List where client data lives: tax software, email, cloud storage, computers, phones and paper.
  2. Write down what protections are in place for each, and what isn't.
  3. Name your Coordinator and set target dates for the gaps.
  4. Train staff, sign the plan and review it at least once a year. The WISP checklist helps you see what's left.
