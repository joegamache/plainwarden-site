WISP template vs. a plan built from your answers

A WISP template and a plan built from your answers can both work. What the FTC rule and IRS guidance expect either one to show, and how to choose.

There are two common ways to get a written information security plan on paper. You can start from a template, such as the IRS sample in Publication 5708, and edit it until it describes your practice. Or you can answer questions about how your firm works and have a plan drafted from those answers. Either can produce a good plan. Either can produce a bad one.

What matters is not where the words came from. It's whether the plan describes what your firm really does, and what it will fix.

What any plan has to show

The FTC Safeguards Rule requires a written information security program "appropriate to your size and complexity, the nature and scope of your activities, and the sensitivity of" the customer information you handle (16 CFR 314.3(a)). The IRS puts it the same way: "There is no one-size-fits-all WISP," and a sole practitioner can use a shorter, simpler plan than a 10-partner firm (IR-2024-308; Publication 5708). The IRS also says a plan protects client information best when it is tailored to the firm (Tax Tip 2026-49).

The rule ties the pieces together in a set order (16 CFR 314.4):

  1. Assess your risks. The program must be based on a risk assessment that identifies reasonably foreseeable risks to client information (314.4(b)).
  2. Put safeguards in place for those risks. The rule says to "design and implement safeguards to control the risks you identify through risk assessment" (314.4(c)).
  3. Check that they work. Regularly test or otherwise monitor your key controls (314.4(d)).
  4. Adjust. Evaluate and adjust the program in light of that testing and of material changes to your operations (314.4(g)).

A plan that follows this order reads as a description of your practice: these are our risks, this is what we do about each, this is what's still open. That is the test for any WISP, whichever way it was written.

If your firm keeps information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, 16 CFR 314.6 excepts you from a few written pieces: the written risk assessment, the continuous monitoring or testing requirement, the written incident response plan and the yearly written report to a board or senior officer. You still have to assess your risks and build your safeguards on them. The Safeguards Rule article goes through each element.

Starting from a template

Publication 5708 is free, written for tax and accounting firms, and organized around the sections a small practice needs. See what Publication 5708 covers. It is honest about its limits: it says it "is not an exhaustive discussion of everything related to WISPs" and isn't a substitute for a plan based on your firm's own needs and requirements.

A template works well when:

  • You have a few hours to read it sentence by sentence.
  • You're comfortable deciding which statements are true for you and rewriting the ones that aren't.
  • You'll keep a separate list of what you still need to fix.

The common problem is a template that is filled in but not changed. A plan that says every laptop is encrypted, every login uses two-step verification and staff are trained each year, when none of that is true yet, describes a firm that doesn't exist. Watch for:

  • Bracketed placeholders, such as "[Your Firm Name Here]," still in the text.
  • Safeguards you don't have, written as if they were in place.
  • No list of gaps, owners or target dates.
  • Tools, vendors or roles that aren't yours.
  • No date for the next review. The IRS says to regularly review and update the plan as your firm's size, scope and complexity change (Tax Tip 2026-49).

Building from your answers

A question-based approach starts from the facts instead: how many people work in the firm, where client files are stored, which tax software and email you use, whether two-step login is on, how you back up. The plan is drafted from those answers, so it starts closer to your practice. Answers that point to a missing safeguard become a gap to fix rather than a sentence claiming it's done.

It still depends on you. A plan built from inaccurate answers is no better than an unedited template. Read the draft, correct anything that's wrong, and make sure the person named as your Qualified Individual (16 CFR 314.4(a)) agrees with it.

If someone asks to see your plan

Whoever reads your plan, whether a cyber insurer's application reviewer, a client, a lender or a regulator, it should match what you'd tell them if they asked how you protect client data. Some practical checks:

  • It names a person. One Qualified Individual oversees the program (16 CFR 314.4(a)). Publication 5708's sample calls this role the Data Security Coordinator.
  • It matches your other paperwork. If a cyber insurance application or renewal asks whether you use two-step login, encryption or backups, your answers and your plan should agree. What insurers ask varies; read your own application and policy, and ask your broker if a question is unclear.
  • It shows gaps honestly. A short list of open items with owners and dates is more believable than a plan with no weaknesses.
  • It has a breach plan. The IRS recommends a data theft response plan alongside the WISP (Tax Tip 2026-49). Firms with 5,000 or more consumers need a written incident response plan under 16 CFR 314.4(h). A short tabletop walk-through helps either way.
  • It is dated and reviewed. Note when it was last reviewed and when the next review is due.

A plan documents your practices and gaps. It doesn't make a firm compliant on its own, and neither a template nor a question-based tool can certify that. Whether your setup meets the rule depends on your facts; if you're unsure, check with your adviser.

Which to choose

  • Choose the template if you have the time, like working from a blank structure, and want the free IRS version. Use the WISP checklist to see what's left.
  • Choose a question-based plan if you'd rather describe your practice than edit a long document, and want gaps listed for you.

Whichever you pick, fix the biggest gaps first. Two-step login, encryption and vendor oversight come up for almost every practice; see turn on two-step login, encrypt computers, phones and drives and the vendor security guide. Working from home? See Do I need a WISP if I work from home?
